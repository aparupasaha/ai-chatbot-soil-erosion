# G. Technique Comparisons

## Difference between contour farming and terracing (contour farming vs terracing)
- **Nature:** Contour farming is an agronomic practice – field operations and crop rows follow the contour; it does not change the shape of the land. Terracing is a mechanical/engineering measure – earthworks (ridges and channels or step-like benches) are built that permanently reshape the slope.
- **How they work:** Contour farming creates small ridges and furrows across the slope that slow and hold runoff. Terracing reduces the length and/or steepness of the slope itself, so runoff cannot build up velocity, and may convey excess water to safe outlets.
- **Slope suitability:** Contour farming is most effective on gentle to moderate, fairly uniform slopes (commonly cited as about 2–10%). Terracing (especially bench terracing) is used on steeper slopes where contour farming alone cannot control runoff.
- **Cost and effort:** Contour farming is low-cost, needs no construction and can be adopted every season. Terracing requires survey, design, significant labour and investment, and ongoing maintenance of risers and outlets.
- **Degree and permanence of protection:** Contour farming's small ridges can be overtopped or broken in intense storms. Terraces give more permanent, robust control on steep land.
- **Combination:** They are complementary – on terraced land, cultivation is still done along the contour. Which is appropriate depends on slope, soil, rainfall and resources; neither is universally better.

## Mulching vs cover crops
- **What they are:** Mulching places a layer of (usually dead) material – residues, straw, leaves, plastic – on the soil surface. Cover cropping grows living plants to cover the soil between or alongside main crops.
- **Protection mechanism:** Both absorb raindrop impact, slow runoff and reduce wind at the soil surface. Mulch gives immediate protection as soon as it is applied; cover crops protect only after they have established, but they also add living roots that bind soil and improve structure.
- **Soil improvement:** Organic mulch adds organic matter as it decomposes; cover crops add both roots and shoots, and legume covers fix nitrogen.
- **Resources:** Mulching needs mulch material to be available and transported and labour to spread it; cover crops need seed, moisture and management (sowing and termination) and may compete for water in dry areas.
- **Best-fit situations:** Mulch is useful where immediate cover is needed (newly seeded or disturbed areas, dry areas for moisture conservation, orchards and vegetables). Cover crops suit fallow periods with enough moisture. The two are often combined – a terminated cover crop becomes a mulch.

## Windbreaks vs shelterbelts
- **Similarity:** Both are barriers of trees and/or shrubs planted across the prevailing wind to reduce wind velocity and control wind erosion, and the terms are often used interchangeably.
- **Size and structure:** A windbreak is usually narrower – one or a few rows of trees, shrubs or even non-living barriers – and often protects a particular field, orchard, farmstead or livestock area. A shelterbelt is generally a wider belt of several rows of trees and shrubs of different heights, planned to protect larger areas of cropland or settlements.
- **Effect:** Both reduce wind speed on the leeward side over a distance proportional to their height; shelterbelts, being wider and multi-layered, create a denser profile from the ground to the tree tops and also provide more timber, fuel, fodder and habitat.
- **Considerations:** Shelterbelts take more land and longer to establish; windbreaks take less space but give protection over a smaller area. The choice depends on the area to be protected, wind conditions and available land.

## Conservation tillage vs conventional tillage
- **Soil disturbance:** Conventional tillage typically involves primary tillage such as mouldboard or disc ploughing followed by several secondary operations, inverting and loosening the soil. Conservation tillage (including no-till, strip-till, ridge-till and mulch-till) minimises disturbance.
- **Residue cover:** Conventional tillage buries most residue, leaving the surface largely bare (commonly less than 15% cover). Conservation tillage leaves at least 30% of the surface covered with residue after planting (USDA definition).
- **Erosion:** Because of the bare, loose surface, conventional tillage leaves soil highly susceptible to water and wind erosion; conservation tillage greatly reduces erosion by protecting the surface and preserving aggregates and infiltration.
- **Other effects:** Conservation tillage conserves moisture, saves fuel, labour and time, and builds organic matter; conventional tillage gives a clean seedbed, buries weeds and residues, and helps warm and dry soils – which can be an advantage on poorly drained, cold soils – but causes faster organic matter loss and can form plough pans.
- **Considerations:** Conservation tillage requires suitable seeding equipment and changes in weed and residue management. The suitable system depends on soil, climate, crop and resources.

## Contour bunding vs graded bunding
- **Alignment:** Contour bunds are built exactly along the contour (level, no longitudinal gradient). Graded bunds are built with a small, uniform gradient along their length (commonly about 0.2–0.4%) and have a channel on the upslope side.
- **Purpose:** Contour bunds are designed to hold (impound) runoff so it infiltrates – they are moisture-conservation structures. Graded bunds are designed to safely drain excess runoff to an outlet – they are runoff-disposal structures.
- **Where used:** Contour bunding suits low-rainfall areas (in Indian practice generally below about 600 mm) with permeable soils. Graded bunding suits higher-rainfall areas (above about 600 mm) and/or soils with low permeability, where impounding water would cause waterlogging or bund breaching.
- **Outlets:** Both need safe arrangements for excess water, but graded bunds depend on a grassed waterway or other outlet at their end to receive the drained runoff.
- Both shorten the slope length and reduce erosion; the choice depends mainly on rainfall, soil permeability and slope.

## Agronomic vs mechanical conservation methods
- **Approach:** Agronomic methods use crops, crop management and surface cover (crop rotation, strip cropping, cover crops, contour farming, conservation tillage, mulching, intercropping, residue management). Mechanical methods use earthworks and structures (bunds, terraces, trenches, check dams, waterways, diversion drains).
- **Mechanism:** Agronomic methods mainly protect the soil from raindrop impact and wind, increase infiltration and improve soil structure – they act at the source of detachment. Mechanical methods mainly control the flow of runoff – shortening and flattening slopes, storing water and safely disposing of excess runoff.
- **Cost and effort:** Agronomic methods are generally low-cost and done each season as part of farming. Mechanical methods involve higher initial investment, technical survey and design, and maintenance, but they are long-lasting.
- **Suitability:** Agronomic methods alone are often sufficient on gentle slopes and under moderate rainfall. Mechanical measures become necessary on steeper slopes, with heavy rainfall and runoff, or where gullies have formed.
- **Additional benefits:** Agronomic methods also improve soil fertility and yields; mechanical methods can support water harvesting and cultivation of steep land.
- **Integration:** The two are complementary rather than competing: mechanical structures are more effective and last longer when supported by vegetation and good crop management, and agronomic practices are more reliable when runoff is controlled by structures.

## Vegetative vs mechanical methods
Vegetative methods (grass cover, vegetative barriers, trees, windbreaks, buffer strips) use living plants to protect and bind the soil; they are cheaper, self-renewing and provide products such as fodder and fuel, but take time to establish and can compete with crops. Mechanical methods (bunds, terraces, check dams, waterways) give immediate, engineered control of runoff and are needed on steep land and in gullies, but cost more and need maintenance. Mechanical structures are commonly stabilised with vegetation (e.g. grass on bunds and terrace risers, grassed waterways), so the two are usually combined.
