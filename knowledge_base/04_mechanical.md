# C. Mechanical / Engineering Techniques

## Mechanical (engineering) methods of soil conservation - overview
Mechanical or engineering methods use earthworks and structures to control the movement of water over the land. They work by (1) breaking long slopes into shorter segments so runoff cannot build up velocity, (2) reducing the effective slope of the land, (3) holding water on the land so it can infiltrate, and (4) safely diverting and disposing of excess runoff through stable channels and outlets. The main mechanical methods are: contour bunding, graded bunding, terracing (including bench terraces), contour trenches, check dams, gully plugging, grassed waterways, diversion drains, water-harvesting structures and sediment traps. They are used where agronomic and vegetative measures alone are not enough – on steeper slopes, in high-rainfall areas, or where gullies have formed. They require design, investment and regular maintenance, and they work best when combined with vegetation and good crop management.

## Contour bunding
**Definition:** Contour bunding is the construction of small earthen embankments (bunds) along the contour lines of a field, at suitable vertical intervals, with the bund level (no gradient) along its length.
**Basic working principle:** Each bund acts as a barrier across the slope that intercepts runoff and holds it in the area above the bund, allowing it to soak into the soil. The slope length is divided into a series of short segments.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** By dividing a long slope into shorter sections, runoff cannot gain the velocity needed to cause serious erosion; water held behind bunds infiltrates, conserving moisture; eroded soil is deposited behind the bund.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Low-rainfall areas (in Indian practice, generally below about 600 mm annual rainfall) with permeable soils, on gentle to moderate slopes (commonly up to about 6%). Suitable for dryland farming where moisture conservation is important.
**Advantages:** Conserves both soil and moisture, relatively simple and low-cost earthwork, can be built with local labour, improves yields in dry areas.
**Limitations/considerations:** Not suitable for soils with poor permeability or high-rainfall areas, because stored water causes waterlogging and bunds may breach; bunds occupy some land; need surplussing arrangements (outlets) for excess water and regular maintenance; stabilising bunds with grass is recommended.

## Graded bunding
**Definition:** Graded bunding (channel terracing) is the construction of bunds along a line with a small, uniform longitudinal gradient instead of exactly on the contour, with a channel on the upslope side.
**Basic working principle:** Instead of holding all the water, the graded bund and its channel collect runoff and carry excess water slowly across the slope to a safe outlet (such as a grassed waterway) at a non-erosive velocity. The channel gradient is kept small (commonly in the range of about 0.2–0.4%).
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Breaks the slope into shorter lengths and safely disposes of excess runoff without allowing it to flow straight downhill and form rills and gullies.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Areas with higher rainfall (in Indian practice, above about 600 mm) and/or soils with low permeability (e.g. clayey black soils), where holding water behind level bunds would cause waterlogging.
**Advantages:** Controls erosion while preventing waterlogging and bund breaching; suitable for heavy soils and wetter regions.
**Limitations/considerations:** Requires careful survey and layout of gradients; needs stable outlets or grassed waterways; conserves less moisture than contour bunds; requires maintenance of channels.

## Terracing
**Definition:** Terracing is the construction of embankments or ridges and channels (or level step-like platforms) across the slope of the land to break a long slope into a series of shorter, flatter segments.
**Basic working principle:** A terrace reduces the length and/or the steepness of the slope. Runoff is either held on the terrace to infiltrate (level/absorptive terraces) or carried across the slope at a non-erosive gradient to a safe outlet (graded/drainage terraces).
**Types:** (1) Broad-base terraces (ridge-and-channel terraces) – broad, low embankments on gentle slopes that can be farmed over; they may be level or graded. (2) Bench terraces – a series of nearly level platforms (steps) cut into steep slopes (see Bench terraces).
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Shortening the slope length and reducing the land slope decreases runoff velocity and its erosive power; terraces trap sediment and increase infiltration.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Sloping cultivated land that is too steep or long for contour farming or bunding alone; bench terraces on steep hill slopes (e.g. the Himalayan and Western Ghats regions, Southeast Asian rice terraces).
**Advantages:** Permanent and highly effective control of water erosion on sloping land, allows cultivation of steep land, conserves moisture, enables irrigation on hill slopes.
**Limitations/considerations:** High construction cost and labour; requires technical design; may expose less fertile subsoil during construction; risers and outlets need maintenance and protection with vegetation; poorly maintained terraces can fail and cause severe gullying.

## Bench terraces
**Definition:** Bench terraces are a series of level or nearly level strips (platforms or "benches") constructed across steep slopes, separated by vertical or near-vertical risers, forming a staircase-like profile.
**Basic working principle:** The steep slope is converted into a series of flat steps; each bench holds rainfall so that it infiltrates, and the riser is protected with stone or grass.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** The land slope on each bench is nearly zero, so runoff velocity is minimal; slope length is limited to the width of one bench; water infiltrates rather than flowing downhill.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Steep slopes (in Indian practice commonly about 16–33% slope) with adequate soil depth, especially in hilly regions; widely used for rice and horticulture in hills. Types include level (table-top) benches, outward-sloping and inward-sloping benches (the latter drain towards the hill side, suited to high rainfall).
**Advantages:** Makes steep land cultivable, very effective against water erosion, allows irrigation and paddy cultivation.
**Limitations/considerations:** Very labour- and cost-intensive; needs deep soil; risers must be stabilised and maintained; unsuitable for very shallow or unstable soils; improper drainage can cause slips.

## Contour trenches
**Definition:** Contour trenches are trenches (ditches) dug along the contour lines of a slope, usually at regular intervals, with the excavated soil formed into a bund on the downslope side. They may be continuous or staggered (discontinuous).
**Basic working principle:** The trenches intercept and store runoff flowing down the slope and allow it to infiltrate into the soil.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Break the slope length, trap runoff and sediment, and increase groundwater recharge and soil moisture for plants growing near the trench.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Non-arable land, hill slopes, degraded and forest land, and afforestation sites (trees are often planted on the bund or near the trench).
**Advantages:** Low-cost, uses local labour, improves moisture for tree establishment, promotes groundwater recharge.
**Limitations/considerations:** Must be laid accurately on the contour, otherwise water concentrates at low points and breaches; trenches silt up and need periodic cleaning; generally not used on cultivated fields.

## Check dams
**Definition:** Check dams are small, low barriers built across gullies, drainage lines or small streams, using materials such as loose stones (boulders), brushwood, earth, gabions (stone-filled wire mesh), sandbags or masonry/concrete.
**What check dams are used for / basic working principle:** They reduce the gradient of the flow channel and slow down the velocity of water flowing in the gully. The water temporarily ponds behind the dam, so the sediment it carries is deposited.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Lower flow velocity reduces the scouring of the gully bed and banks, stops the gully from deepening and extending upstream, traps sediment, and increases infiltration and groundwater recharge. Over time the gully bed fills with sediment and can be vegetated.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Gullies and small drainage lines in agricultural and non-agricultural catchments; usually as a series of structures along a gully.
**Advantages:** Stabilises gullies, traps sediment, recharges groundwater, can store water for use; temporary types are cheap and use local materials.
**Limitations/considerations:** Must be designed with an adequate spillway and keyed into the banks and bed to prevent undercutting and bypassing; temporary structures have a limited life; permanent structures are costly; they fill with sediment and need maintenance; should be combined with catchment treatment.

## Gully plugging
**Definition:** Gully plugging (gully control) is the treatment of gullies by placing small structures (plugs) such as brushwood dams, loose boulder plugs, earthen plugs, sandbag plugs or vegetative plugs across the gully bed, usually in a series from the head downwards.
**Basic working principle:** The plugs reduce the velocity of water in the gully, cause sediment to be deposited behind them, and gradually raise and stabilise the gully bed so vegetation can grow.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Prevents further deepening and widening of the gully and stops the gully head from advancing uphill; trapped sediment and vegetation eventually heal the gully.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Small to medium gullies in cropland, grazing land and forest land; larger gullies need engineering structures such as check dams, drop structures and diversion of runoff away from the gully head.
**Advantages:** Low-cost, uses local material and labour, reclaims land, reduces sediment delivery downstream.
**Limitations/considerations:** Needs regular inspection and repair after heavy flows; must be combined with treatment of the catchment above the gully (diverting runoff, vegetation) for permanent control; gully head stabilisation is critical.

## Grassed waterways
**Definition:** A grassed waterway is a natural or constructed broad, shallow channel with a permanent cover of suitable grass, designed to carry concentrated runoff from fields, terraces or diversions to a safe outlet without causing erosion.
**Basic working principle:** The grass lining protects the channel bed from scouring and slows the water velocity; the channel is shaped (commonly parabolic or trapezoidal) and sized to carry the design runoff at a non-erosive velocity.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Prevents concentrated flow from forming gullies in natural drainage lines; filters sediment from runoff; serves as a safe outlet for graded bunds and terraces.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Natural drainage ways in cropland where water concentrates, and as outlets for terraces, graded bunds and diversions.
**Advantages:** Prevents gully formation, stable and relatively low-cost disposal of runoff, can provide fodder, improves water quality.
**Limitations/considerations:** Must be established (grass fully grown) before carrying large flows; must not be used as a road or ploughed through; needs mowing, repair of washouts and control of sediment deposits; takes land out of production.

## Diversion drains
**Definition:** Diversion drains (diversion channels or diversions) are channels, usually with a supporting ridge on the downslope side, constructed across the slope to intercept runoff from higher land and carry it away to a safe outlet.
**Basic working principle:** They catch runoff before it reaches vulnerable areas (cropland, gully heads, terraces, farmsteads) and divert it along a gentle gradient to a stable outlet such as a grassed waterway or natural stream.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Reduces the amount of water flowing over the protected area, prevents the formation and advancement of gullies, and protects structures below from being overtopped.
**Where it is generally applicable:** At the foot of hills above cultivated land, above gully heads, above terraced areas, and around farm buildings.
**Advantages:** Protects large areas downstream, simple earthwork, an essential part of a watershed runoff-disposal system.
**Limitations/considerations:** Must have a stable, protected outlet and a non-erosive gradient; channels need vegetation and maintenance to prevent silting or scouring; a failure can release concentrated flow.

## Water-harvesting structures
**Definition:** Water-harvesting structures collect and store rainfall runoff for later use or for groundwater recharge. Examples include farm ponds, percolation tanks, nala (stream) bunds, check dams, micro-catchments around trees (e.g. half-moon and trench structures) and rooftop harvesting.
**Basic working principle:** Runoff from a catchment is intercepted and stored in a pond, tank or soil profile instead of being allowed to flow away, so its energy is dissipated and water becomes available for irrigation, livestock or recharge.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** Reduces the volume and peak rate of runoff moving downslope, traps sediment, and reduces erosion in downstream channels; the stored water supports vegetation that further protects the soil.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Arid and semi-arid rainfed regions and watershed development programmes; ponds and tanks sited at natural drainage points.
**Advantages:** Combines soil conservation with water supply, supports protective irrigation and drought resilience, recharges groundwater.
**Limitations/considerations:** Needs correct siting and design (catchment size, spillway); siltation reduces capacity, so catchment treatment and desilting are needed; seepage and evaporation losses; may require investment.

## Sediment traps
**Definition:** Sediment traps (and sediment basins or water and sediment control basins) are small temporary or permanent structures – such as excavated pits, small embankments with an outlet, or silt fences – that detain runoff so that suspended sediment settles out.
**Basic working principle:** Runoff is briefly ponded, its velocity drops, and soil particles settle before the cleaner water is released through an outlet.
**How it reduces runoff/erosion:** They do not stop erosion at the source but prevent eroded soil from leaving the field or site and from reaching streams, reservoirs and drains; they can also reduce peak flows downstream.
**Where it is generally applicable:** Below cropland, at the outlets of small catchments, construction and mining sites, road sides, and above water-harvesting ponds to reduce their siltation.
**Advantages:** Protect water bodies and structures from siltation, simple to construct, trapped soil can sometimes be returned to fields.
**Limitations/considerations:** Must be cleaned out regularly to remain effective; they treat the symptom (sediment) rather than the cause, so they should be combined with source-control measures such as vegetation and mulching.
