# B. Vegetative / Biological Techniques

## Vegetative (biological) methods of soil conservation - overview
Vegetative or biological methods use permanent or long-lasting vegetation – trees, shrubs and grasses – to protect the soil. Vegetation protects soil in several ways: the canopy and litter intercept raindrops and absorb their energy; stems and litter slow down runoff and trap sediment; roots bind soil particles and create pores that increase infiltration; plant material adds organic matter that stabilises soil aggregates; and tall vegetation reduces wind speed near the ground. The main vegetative methods are afforestation/reforestation, grass cover, vegetative barriers, shelterbelts, windbreaks, agroforestry, pasture/grassland management and riparian vegetation/buffer strips. They are relatively cheap, self-maintaining once established, and give extra products (fodder, fuel, timber, fruit), but they take time to establish and need protection from grazing in the early years.

## Afforestation and reforestation
**What it is:** Afforestation is planting trees on land that has not been forested in recent times (e.g. degraded wasteland, ravines, dunes); reforestation is replanting trees on land where forest has been cleared or degraded.
**How vegetation protects soil:** The tree canopy and the understorey intercept rainfall; the layer of leaf litter on the forest floor absorbs raindrop impact and slows runoff; deep and extensive root systems bind soil and stabilise slopes; forest soils have high organic matter and infiltration capacity; trees reduce wind speed.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Water erosion (sheet, rill, gully erosion and unstable slopes, catchments of reservoirs) and wind erosion (e.g. sand-dune stabilisation in deserts).
**Major benefits:** Long-term protection of catchments and steep lands, reduced sedimentation of reservoirs, improved groundwater recharge, biodiversity, timber, fuelwood and fodder, carbon storage.
**Important considerations:** Takes years to become effective; species must suit the local climate and soil (native species are preferred); seedlings need protection from grazing and fire; the forest floor litter must not be removed, because a tree canopy alone without ground cover gives much less protection.

## Grass cover
**What it is:** Establishing and maintaining a dense cover of grasses (and grass–legume mixtures) on erodible land such as steep slopes, bunds, terrace risers, waterways, embankments and degraded land.
**How vegetation protects soil:** A dense grass sward covers the soil surface completely; its fibrous root mat binds the topsoil very effectively; the many stems create high surface roughness, slowing runoff and filtering out sediment.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Sheet and rill erosion by water, erosion of channels and structures (grassed waterways, bund faces), and wind erosion.
**Major benefits:** One of the most effective and cheapest covers for erosion control; provides fodder; improves soil organic matter and structure.
**Important considerations:** Must be protected from overgrazing and fire; species selection depends on climate (e.g. drought-tolerant species in dry areas); needs time to establish, so newly seeded areas may need mulch.

## Vegetative barriers
**What it is:** Vegetative barriers (vegetative hedges) are narrow, dense, permanent strips of stiff-stemmed grasses or shrubs planted along the contour of sloping land. A well-known example is vetiver grass (Chrysopogon zizanioides) hedges; other grasses and shrubs are also used.
**How vegetation protects soil:** The dense, stiff stems act as a living filter across the slope: they slow down runoff, cause water to spread out and pond behind the barrier, and trap the sediment it carries. Over time the deposited sediment builds up behind the barrier, forming natural terraces. Deep roots also stabilise the barrier itself.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Sheet and rill erosion by water on sloping cropland; they are sometimes used as a low-cost alternative or supplement to earthen bunds.
**Major benefits:** Low cost compared with earthworks, self-repairing, gradual formation of terraces, provides fodder or thatch (depending on species), moisture conservation.
**Important considerations:** Must be established on the contour with no gaps, otherwise runoff concentrates through gaps; take one or more seasons to become effective; occupy some cropland; the species should not become a weed or harbour pests.

## Shelterbelts
**What it is:** A shelterbelt is a wide belt of several rows of trees and shrubs of different heights planted across the direction of the prevailing wind, usually to protect large areas of cropland, farmsteads and villages.
**How vegetation protects soil:** The belt acts as a barrier that deflects wind upward and slows the wind speed on the leeward (downwind) side and, to a smaller extent, on the windward side. Lower wind speed near the ground means less soil detachment and less movement of soil particles by saltation, suspension and creep.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Wind erosion (particularly in arid and semi-arid regions, e.g. western Rajasthan in India, the Great Plains of the USA).
**Major benefits:** Reduced wind erosion and sand movement, reduced evaporation and crop damage (desiccation, lodging, sandblasting), improved microclimate, fuelwood, timber and fodder, wildlife habitat.
**Important considerations:** A typical design has tall trees in the centre and shorter trees and shrubs on the edges to give a dense profile from the ground up; must be oriented perpendicular to the prevailing wind; takes several years to grow; occupies land and trees compete with adjacent crops for water and nutrients near the belt; gaps in the belt create funnels of high wind.

## Windbreaks
**What it is:** A windbreak is a barrier of one or a few rows of trees, shrubs or other plants (and sometimes non-living barriers such as fences or walls) placed across the prevailing wind to reduce wind speed. The terms windbreak and shelterbelt are often used interchangeably; windbreaks are usually narrower (one to a few rows) and may protect smaller areas such as fields, orchards or homesteads.
**Role of windbreaks / how windbreaks control wind erosion:** A windbreak slows the wind by forcing part of it to rise over the barrier and by filtering the rest through it. This creates a zone of reduced wind velocity on the leeward side. The extent of protection is proportional to the height (H) of the windbreak; protection is commonly described as extending downwind roughly 10 times the height, with smaller reductions further out depending on design. Because the wind speed near the ground falls below the threshold needed to lift soil particles, fewer particles are detached and moved. A moderately dense (porous) windbreak gives a longer protected zone and less turbulence than a completely solid barrier.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Wind erosion; also protects crops and livestock from wind damage.
**Major benefits:** Reduced soil blowing, lower evaporation and crop water loss, protection of crops from mechanical damage, snow trapping in cold areas, improved yields in the sheltered zone, products such as timber and fruit.
**Important considerations:** Must be perpendicular to the prevailing erosive wind; density (porosity), height, length and continuity determine effectiveness; takes years to establish; competes with nearby crops; needs maintenance and replacement of dead plants.

## Agroforestry
**What it is:** Agroforestry is a land-use system in which trees or shrubs are deliberately grown together with crops and/or livestock on the same piece of land. Forms include alley cropping (crops grown between rows of trees or hedgerows), silvopasture (trees with pasture and livestock), boundary and contour tree planting, and windbreaks on farms.
**How vegetation protects soil:** Trees provide a canopy and leaf litter that protect the soil surface; tree roots bind soil and improve infiltration; hedgerows planted along the contour act as barriers that trap sediment and gradually form terraces; prunings can be used as mulch; trees reduce wind speed.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Water erosion on sloping land and wind erosion in open, dry landscapes.
**Major benefits:** Erosion control combined with production of food, fodder, fuelwood, timber and fruit; improved soil fertility (especially with nitrogen-fixing trees); diversified income; carbon storage.
**Important considerations:** Competition between trees and crops for light, water and nutrients must be managed (pruning, spacing); longer time to return; requires suitable species selection and knowledge.

## Pasture and grassland management
**What it is:** Management of grazing lands and grasslands to keep a dense, productive plant cover – through controlled stocking rates, rotational grazing, rest periods, reseeding of degraded areas and protection from fire.
**How vegetation protects soil:** A healthy, dense grass cover and litter layer protect the soil from raindrops and wind and bind it with roots. Overgrazing removes this cover, compacts soil by trampling, and creates cattle tracks that develop into rills and gullies.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Sheet, rill and gully erosion by water and wind erosion on rangelands and pastures.
**Major benefits:** Maintains soil cover and fertility, sustains fodder production for livestock, improves infiltration, prevents land degradation and desertification.
**Important considerations:** Requires matching animal numbers to the carrying capacity of the land; needs fencing, water points or community agreements for rotational grazing; degraded pastures may need reseeding and temporary closure.

## Riparian vegetation and buffer strips
**What it is:** Riparian buffers are strips of permanent vegetation (trees, shrubs and grasses) maintained along the banks of streams, rivers, lakes and ponds. Buffer or filter strips are also placed at field edges and below cropland to intercept runoff before it reaches water bodies.
**How vegetation protects soil:** Roots of trees and shrubs hold stream banks together and resist bank scouring; the dense vegetation slows runoff from adjacent fields, causes sediment to be deposited within the strip, and increases infiltration; the vegetation also absorbs nutrients and filters pollutants.
**Type of erosion controlled:** Stream-bank erosion and sediment delivery from sheet and rill erosion on adjacent land.
**Major benefits:** Stable banks, cleaner water (less sediment, nutrients and pesticides), shade and habitat for aquatic life, wildlife corridors, flood-flow moderation.
**Important considerations:** Width must be adequate for the slope and runoff; concentrated flows can cut through a buffer, so runoff should enter as sheet flow; takes land out of production; needs maintenance and protection from grazing.
