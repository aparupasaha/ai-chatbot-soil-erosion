# E. Wind-Erosion Control

## Methods that control wind erosion - overview
Wind erosion occurs when strong wind blows over soil that is dry, loose, finely divided, bare and smooth, and when the field is wide and unsheltered in the wind direction. Soil particles move by saltation (bouncing), suspension (fine dust) and surface creep (rolling), and saltating particles knock loose more particles as they land (abrasion), so erosion increases with the unprotected distance across a field. Wind-erosion control methods work on the following principles: reduce wind velocity near the ground; keep the soil surface covered; increase surface roughness to trap moving particles; keep the soil moist and well aggregated (cloddy); and shorten the unsheltered field length in the wind direction. The major practices are:
- **Windbreaks** – rows of trees/shrubs across the prevailing wind that reduce wind speed on the leeward side.
- **Shelterbelts** – wider, multi-row tree belts protecting large areas.
- **Vegetative cover** – living plants shield the soil and slow wind at the surface.
- **Maintaining crop residues** – standing stubble and flat residues reduce surface wind speed and trap soil.
- **Strip cropping** – alternating strips of crops across the wind trap moving soil and reduce the unsheltered distance.
- **Mulching** – covers and holds the soil and conserves moisture.
- **Afforestation** – permanent tree cover, including stabilisation of sand dunes.
- **Reducing exposed/bare soil** – avoiding fallow periods without cover, overgrazing and excessive tillage.
Other supporting practices include conservation tillage/no-till, ridging or rough tillage across the wind direction (to create clods and ridges that trap particles), and controlled grazing.

## How each wind-erosion practice reduces wind velocity, soil detachment or particle movement
- **Windbreaks and shelterbelts** act as barriers that deflect the wind upward and filter it, reducing wind velocity for a distance downwind that is proportional to their height (commonly described as roughly 10 times the height of the barrier). Below the threshold velocity, soil particles are not lifted, so detachment and movement are reduced; the barrier also traps drifting soil.
- **Vegetative cover** absorbs the force of the wind (the wind acts on the plants instead of the soil), increases surface roughness so wind speed at the soil surface is low, and roots bind the soil against detachment.
- **Crop residues** – standing stubble is especially effective because it raises the height at which wind velocity is reduced; flat residues cover the soil and physically prevent particles from being picked up; both trap saltating particles and so stop the chain reaction of abrasion.
- **Strip cropping** (wind strip cropping) places protective strips at right angles to the wind; soil moving from an exposed strip is caught in the next protected strip, and the width of exposed soil is kept short so soil movement cannot build up across the field.
- **Mulching** covers the soil surface so wind cannot reach the particles, adds roughness, and conserves soil moisture (moist soil is harder for wind to move).
- **Afforestation** creates a permanent cover and large-scale reduction in wind speed; in deserts it is used with grasses and shrubs to stabilise shifting sand dunes.
- **Reducing exposed/bare soil** removes the source area from which wind can lift particles; bare, loose, dry soil is the most vulnerable to wind erosion.

## Reducing exposed or bare soil
Bare soil is the most vulnerable condition for both wind and water erosion. Exposure can be reduced by keeping crops, cover crops or residues on the field throughout the year, avoiding long bare fallows, not burning or removing residues, minimising tillage (especially in dry, windy seasons), preventing overgrazing, and quickly re-vegetating disturbed areas such as construction sites, roadsides and degraded land. Where bare soil cannot be avoided, mulching, rough tillage across the wind direction or temporary barriers can reduce erosion until cover is established.
