# A. Agronomic / Crop-Management Techniques

## Agronomic methods of soil conservation - overview
Agronomic (crop-management) methods control erosion by the way crops are grown and the soil surface is managed, rather than by building structures. They work mainly by (1) keeping the soil covered by a crop canopy, residues or mulch so raindrops and wind cannot detach particles, (2) increasing surface roughness and infiltration so less water runs off, and (3) improving soil organic matter and structure. The main agronomic methods are: crop rotation, strip cropping, cover cropping, contour farming, conservation tillage, reduced tillage, no-till farming, mulching, intercropping and crop-residue management. They are low-cost, can be adopted by individual farmers, and are most effective on gentle to moderate slopes; on steeper land they are combined with mechanical measures such as bunds and terraces.

## Crop rotation
**Definition:** Crop rotation is the practice of growing different crops in a planned sequence on the same field over successive seasons or years, instead of growing the same crop repeatedly (monocropping).
**How it works:** A rotation alternates erosion-permitting crops (row crops such as maize, cotton, tobacco, potato that leave much soil exposed) with erosion-resisting crops (close-growing cereals, legumes, grasses, forages) that cover the soil densely.
**How it reduces erosion:** Close-growing and sod crops protect the soil for part of the rotation, add roots and residues that build organic matter, and improve soil aggregation and infiltration, so the average soil loss over the whole rotation is lower than under continuous row cropping. Legumes also add nitrogen.
**Suitable conditions:** Almost all arable farming systems; especially useful where row crops are grown on sloping land.
**Advantages:** Reduces erosion, improves soil structure and fertility, breaks pest, disease and weed cycles, diversifies farm income.
**Limitations/considerations:** Requires planning and markets for several crops; the benefit depends on the proportion of time the soil is under protective crops; alone it may not be enough on steep slopes.

## Strip cropping
**Definition:** Strip cropping is growing crops in long, relatively narrow alternating strips, where strips of erosion-permitting row crops alternate with strips of erosion-resisting close-growing crops (grasses, legumes, small grains).
**How it works:** On slopes the strips are laid across the slope; when water flowing from a row-crop strip reaches the dense strip below, it is slowed, part of it infiltrates and the sediment is filtered out. For wind erosion the strips are laid perpendicular to the prevailing wind so the protective strips trap moving soil and reduce the unsheltered distance across which wind can pick up particles.
**Types:** (1) Contour strip cropping – strips follow the contour; (2) Field strip cropping – strips of uniform width across the general slope, used where the topography is too irregular for contour strips; (3) Wind strip cropping – strips at right angles to the prevailing wind; (4) Buffer strip cropping – strips of grass or legumes placed between contour strips on steep or badly eroded parts.
**How it reduces erosion:** Shortens the effective slope length or wind-exposed field width, slows runoff, traps sediment, and provides surface protection over part of the field at all times.
**Suitable conditions:** Moderate slopes with fairly uniform topography for contour strips; flat, windy dryland areas for wind strip cropping.
**Advantages:** Controls both water and wind erosion, low cost, fits with crop rotation (strips are rotated).
**Limitations/considerations:** Narrow strips can complicate the use of large machinery; strip widths must be matched to slope and wind; on steep slopes it needs support from terraces or bunds.

## Cover cropping
**Definition:** Cover crops are crops (such as legumes, grasses, cereals like rye or oats, or mixtures) grown mainly to protect and improve the soil rather than for harvest, often during the fallow period between two main crops.
**How it works:** The cover crop canopy and residues shield the soil from raindrops and wind, while roots bind soil particles and create channels that improve infiltration.
**How it reduces erosion:** Prevents the soil from being bare during off-seasons when erosion risk is high, reduces runoff, and increases organic matter and aggregate stability. Both water and wind erosion are reduced.
**Suitable conditions:** Where there is a gap between main crops with enough moisture for the cover crop to establish; widely used in orchards and plantations as a ground cover.
**Advantages:** Erosion control, improved soil organic matter and structure, nitrogen fixation (legumes), weed suppression, nutrient capture (reduced leaching).
**Limitations/considerations:** Seed and establishment cost; may use soil moisture needed by the next crop in dry areas; must be terminated at the right time; can host some pests.

## Contour farming
**Definition:** Contour farming (contour cultivation) means carrying out all field operations – ploughing, planting, cultivating and harvesting – across the slope, following the contour lines (lines of equal elevation), instead of up and down the slope.
**How it works:** The furrows, ridges and plant rows formed across the slope act as a series of small barriers or reservoirs. Water collects in the furrows and has time to soak in rather than running directly downhill.
**How it reduces erosion:** It reduces the velocity and volume of runoff and therefore its ability to detach and carry soil; it increases infiltration and conserves moisture. Up-and-down-slope rows, in contrast, act as channels that speed up runoff and form rills.
**Suitable conditions:** Most effective on gentle to moderate slopes with fairly uniform topography (commonly cited as roughly 2–10% slope). On steeper or long slopes, heavy storms can overtop the ridges, so it is combined with strip cropping, bunds or terraces.
**Advantages:** Simple, low cost, no special equipment or earthwork, conserves both soil and water, can increase yields in dry areas through moisture conservation.
**Limitations/considerations:** Less effective on steep, irregular or very long slopes and during intense storms, when rows can break and concentrate flow; requires laying out contour guidelines; can make machinery operation slightly less convenient.

## Conservation tillage
**Definition:** Conservation tillage is any tillage and planting system that leaves a substantial amount of the previous crop's residue on the soil surface. The widely used USDA definition is a system that leaves at least 30% of the soil surface covered with residue after planting. It includes no-till, strip-till, ridge-till and mulch-till.
**How it works:** Soil disturbance is reduced, and residues are left on the surface instead of being buried by inversion ploughing.
**How it reduces erosion:** The surface residue absorbs raindrop impact, slows runoff and wind at the soil surface, and increases infiltration; reduced disturbance maintains soil aggregates and organic matter, making the soil more resistant to detachment.
**Suitable conditions:** Well-drained soils, both humid and dry regions; particularly valuable on sloping and wind-prone lands.
**Advantages:** Large reductions in water and wind erosion, conserves soil moisture, saves fuel, labour and time, improves soil organic matter and biological activity over time.
**Limitations/considerations:** Needs suitable planting equipment; weed control may depend more on herbicides or other methods; residues can harbour pests and diseases; poorly drained, cold soils may warm and dry more slowly; requires management skill.

## Reduced tillage
**Definition:** Reduced (minimum) tillage is a tillage system that uses fewer or less intensive tillage operations than conventional tillage, limiting soil disturbance to what is needed for crop establishment.
**How it works:** Operations are combined or eliminated (e.g. fewer harrowing passes, shallow tillage instead of deep inversion ploughing), so more residue remains on the surface and the soil is disturbed less.
**How it reduces erosion:** Less pulverisation and exposure of soil, better retention of residues and aggregates, and higher infiltration all reduce runoff and detachment. It may leave less residue than conservation tillage (the 30% threshold is not always met).
**Suitable conditions:** A practical transition step for farmers moving from conventional tillage towards conservation tillage or no-till.
**Advantages:** Lower fuel and labour costs, less soil compaction from repeated passes, reduced erosion compared with conventional tillage.
**Limitations/considerations:** Erosion benefit is smaller than full conservation or no-till systems; weed control needs attention.

## No-till farming
**Definition:** No-till (zero tillage, direct seeding) is a system in which the crop is planted directly into the undisturbed soil and the residue of the previous crop, without any ploughing or seedbed preparation. Only a narrow slot or hole is opened for the seed.
**How it works:** Special planters or drills cut through residue and place seed; weeds are controlled with herbicides, cover crops, or other non-tillage methods.
**How it reduces erosion:** The soil is never loosened and stays covered with residue, so raindrop impact, runoff and wind detachment are greatly reduced; continuous root channels and earthworm burrows improve infiltration. It is one of the most effective agronomic measures against both water and wind erosion.
**Suitable conditions:** Well-drained soils; many cropping systems (e.g. zero-till wheat after rice in the Indo-Gangetic plains, maize and soybean systems in the Americas).
**Advantages:** Maximum residue cover, reduced erosion, water conservation, lower fuel, labour and machinery costs, timelier sowing, improved soil biology and organic matter over time.
**Limitations/considerations:** Requires specialised seeding equipment; dependence on herbicides for weed control; possible surface compaction or slower warming on poorly drained soils; a transition period may be needed before full benefits appear.

## Mulching
**Definition:** Mulching is covering the soil surface with a protective layer of material. Organic mulches include crop residues, straw, leaves, grass clippings and wood chips; inorganic mulches include plastic sheets and gravel.
**How it works:** The mulch layer sits between the atmosphere and the soil: it intercepts raindrops, shields the soil from wind, and creates surface roughness.
**How mulching reduces soil erosion:** (1) It absorbs the energy of falling raindrops, preventing splash detachment and surface sealing/crusting; (2) it slows the flow of runoff over the surface and allows more time for infiltration; (3) it reduces wind velocity at the soil surface and physically holds soil particles in place; (4) organic mulch adds organic matter that improves aggregate stability. It also conserves soil moisture by reducing evaporation and moderates soil temperature.
**Suitable conditions:** Arable fields, orchards, vegetable gardens, newly seeded or disturbed slopes, construction sites; very valuable in dry areas for moisture conservation.
**Advantages:** Immediate protection against both water and wind erosion, moisture conservation, weed suppression, soil temperature moderation, organic matter addition (organic mulch).
**Limitations/considerations:** Availability and cost of mulch material (residues may be needed for fodder or fuel); labour to apply; can harbour pests or rodents; plastic mulch does not add organic matter and must be removed and disposed of; light mulches can be blown away by strong wind unless anchored.

## Intercropping
**Definition:** Intercropping is growing two or more crops simultaneously on the same field, in alternate rows or in a defined pattern (row intercropping, strip intercropping, mixed cropping, relay cropping).
**How it works:** A fast-growing or close-growing crop (often a legume) is grown between rows of a widely spaced main crop, giving more complete and longer-lasting ground cover.
**How it reduces erosion:** The combined canopy covers more soil for more time, intercepts raindrops, and the extra roots bind the soil; rows of the companion crop act as barriers to runoff. Examples include cereals with legumes (e.g. sorghum or maize with pigeonpea, cowpea or groundnut).
**Suitable conditions:** Smallholder and rainfed farming systems; row crops with wide spacing.
**Advantages:** Better ground cover and erosion control, efficient use of land, light and water, reduced risk of total crop failure, nitrogen fixation when legumes are used.
**Limitations/considerations:** Competition between crops for water, light and nutrients; more complex management and harvesting; less suited to fully mechanised operations.

## Crop-residue management
**Definition:** Crop-residue management is the handling of the stalks, stubble, straw and other plant material left after harvest so that it protects the soil, rather than burning, removing or burying it.
**How it works:** Residues are left standing or spread on the surface, or partly incorporated, and the next crop is planted with minimum disturbance (often combined with conservation tillage or no-till).
**How it reduces erosion:** Surface residues absorb raindrop impact, slow runoff and increase infiltration (water erosion); standing stubble and flat residues reduce wind speed at the soil surface and trap moving soil particles (wind erosion). Residues decompose into organic matter that improves soil structure.
**Suitable conditions:** All cropping systems, especially dryland and wind-prone areas and sloping fields after harvest.
**Advantages:** Low-cost protection during the vulnerable period after harvest, moisture conservation, organic matter build-up, avoids the air pollution and nutrient losses caused by residue burning.
**Limitations/considerations:** Competing uses of residue (fodder, fuel, bedding); heavy residues can interfere with sowing unless suitable equipment is used; may carry over some pests and diseases; the amount of cover must be sufficient to be effective.
