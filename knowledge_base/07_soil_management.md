# F. General Soil-Management Practices

## General soil-management practices - overview
General soil-management practices improve the soil's own ability to resist erosion (reducing its erodibility) and keep it protected. A soil with good structure, stable aggregates, high organic matter and good infiltration produces less runoff and is harder for water or wind to detach. The main practices are: organic matter management, compost/manure application, conservation agriculture, maintaining soil structure, controlled grazing, avoiding excessive tillage and maintaining ground cover.

## Organic matter management
Soil organic matter acts as a binding agent (together with roots, fungal hyphae and microbial products) that holds soil particles together in stable aggregates. Well-aggregated soil resists detachment by raindrops and wind, has more pore space, and absorbs water faster, so less runoff is produced. Organic matter also increases water-holding capacity and supports soil organisms such as earthworms whose burrows increase infiltration. It is built up by returning crop residues, growing cover crops and legumes, adding compost and manure, reducing tillage, and avoiding residue burning. Organic matter builds up slowly, so the benefits appear over several years.

## Compost and manure application
Applying compost, farmyard manure, green manure and other organic amendments adds organic matter and nutrients to the soil. This improves aggregate stability, porosity and infiltration, reduces surface crusting, and increases water-holding capacity, making the soil less prone to erosion. Better fertility also produces vigorous crops that cover the soil faster. Considerations: manure and compost should be applied at appropriate rates and incorporated or covered, because manure left on bare, sloping or frozen land can wash off and pollute water; availability and transport costs can be limiting.

## Conservation agriculture
Conservation agriculture (as defined by FAO) is a farming system based on three linked principles: (1) minimum mechanical soil disturbance (no-till or minimum tillage), (2) permanent soil organic cover with crop residues and/or cover crops, and (3) diversification of plant species through crop rotation and associations. Together these principles keep the soil covered and undisturbed, increase organic matter and biological activity, and greatly reduce both water and wind erosion while conserving moisture and reducing costs. Considerations include the need for suitable seeding equipment, new weed-management approaches, competing uses of residues, and a transition period before full benefits.

## Maintaining soil structure
Soil structure is the arrangement of soil particles into aggregates and the pore spaces between them. Good structure allows rapid infiltration and resists detachment; poor structure (due to compaction, loss of organic matter, pulverisation by excessive tillage, or working wet soil) leads to crusting, sealing, reduced infiltration and more runoff and erosion. Structure is maintained by adding organic matter, minimising tillage, avoiding tillage and heavy traffic when the soil is wet, using controlled traffic lanes, keeping living roots in the soil, and preventing overgrazing and trampling.

## Controlled grazing
Controlled grazing (prescribed or rotational grazing) means regulating the number of animals, the duration and the timing of grazing so that vegetation is not overgrazed. Overgrazing removes protective plant cover, weakens root systems, compacts the soil by trampling and creates tracks that become rills and gullies, leading to both water and wind erosion. Controlled grazing keeps a healthy plant and litter cover, allows plants to recover between grazing periods, and maintains infiltration. Methods include matching stocking rates to carrying capacity, rotational grazing between paddocks, resting and closing degraded areas, and keeping animals away from stream banks and steep slopes.

## Avoiding excessive tillage
Excessive or intensive tillage (repeated ploughing and harrowing, especially with inversion ploughs) buries protective residues, breaks down soil aggregates into fine particles, accelerates the loss of organic matter, forms compacted plough pans below the tilled layer, and leaves the surface bare and loose. All of these increase susceptibility to water and wind erosion. Reducing tillage frequency and intensity – through reduced tillage, conservation tillage or no-till – keeps residues on the surface and preserves soil structure. Tillage, when needed, should be done across the slope (on the contour) and at the right soil moisture.

## Maintaining ground cover
Maintaining ground cover means keeping the soil surface covered by living plants, crop residues or mulch for as much of the year as possible. Ground cover is one of the most important factors controlling erosion on most lands: it absorbs raindrop energy, slows runoff, increases infiltration, and prevents wind from reaching the soil. It is achieved through cover crops, crop rotations with close-growing crops, intercropping, residue retention, mulching, conservation tillage, grass on non-cropped areas, and controlled grazing.
